How the three columns are built

Size (first dividend) is the annual growth of the support ratio, effective producers per effective consumer, over the fifteen years ahead. The population moves; each economy's National Transfer Accounts (NTA) labour-income and consumption profiles are held fixed. Clicking the column splits it exactly into three parts by age block: youth (the ages below the surplus years, where consumption exceeds labour income: a falling child share raises the support ratio), working age (the surplus years: a growing working-age population adds producers) and ageing (the ages above the surplus years: a rising old-age share adds consumers). The split is a log-mean decomposition of the change in effective producers less the change in effective consumers, block by block, rescaled by at most 1% so the parts sum to the first dividend.

Intensity (second dividend) follows Mason and Lee, in three steps. First, the pot: for each adult aged 20 and over, add up the years ahead in which they will consume more than they earn, discounted at 3% a year and weighted by the chance of being alive in each of those years (the WPP 2024 life tables); sum over everyone, and express the total in years of the economy's labour income. That is the lifecycle wealth the population needs to have built up to pay for its old age. Second, assume a fixed share of that pot is held as capital at home, so capital per effective producer grows at the same rate as the pot per effective producer. Third, with a capital share of 0.35, output per producer grows at 0.35 times that rate, and that is the column. It covers physical capital only; the human-capital channel is not included. The pot is counted on the silver profile, that is, after the extra working years the third dividend already credits (see below), so the column is the growth of the wealth still needed once people work for as long as the third dividend assumes.

Duration (third dividend) is the extra growth in the support ratio when labour income above its peak age follows prospective old age, the age at which fifteen years of life remain, measured as a shift since 2000 and never allowed to fall. It is the support-ratio growth under that "silver" profile minus the first dividend.

What the second dividend measures, and what it does not

The quantity computed is the wealth people need to hold for old age, not the wealth they hold. That need can be met by pension savings, shares and bonds, housing, assets abroad, or by promises from the state and from children (pay-as-you-go pensions and family transfers, which NTA calls transfer wealth). Only the part that ends up as productive capital at home raises output per worker. The constant-share assumption is what lets the measure be computed; the realised dividend depends on how much of the need is met by saving into domestic capital rather than by transfers or by assets held abroad. Housing is the awkward case: a large part of household wealth, but it adds little to output per worker compared with plant, equipment and infrastructure, and the model treats all capital the same with one capital share.

The measure Part 1's ninth draft used for Figure 9 was simpler: it annuitised today's old-age deficit over life expectancy at 65, so it counted only people who are already old. That put the wealth requirement at a third to a tenth of the Mason-Lee level, but gave a larger dividend, roughly three times the Mason-Lee value, because the dividend is the growth of wealth and a measure that counts ageing only once people are old registers the same change as faster growth. That column was the upper end of the range for the second dividend; the ranking of economies is nearly the same under the two (rank correlation 0.92). Figure 9 is being brought onto the measure used here.

Why the pot is counted after longer working lives

The third dividend assumes that as life expectancy rises, people keep earning for longer: the age at which labour income tails off moves out with the age at which fifteen years of life remain. That is the "silver" profile. The second dividend is about the pot of wealth needed to cover the years of deficit in old age. If people work for longer, those deficit years are fewer and the pot is smaller. So the two dividends, each computed on its own, are two ways of paying for the same old age, by holding assets or by working longer, and the pot counted on today's earning profile includes years that the third dividend has already paid for with work.

The page therefore counts the pot on the silver profile. The third dividend takes what longer working lives cover; the second is 0.35 times the growth of the wealth still needed after that. The two then describe the same future and add without overlap, so the full potential is first plus second plus third. The order is a convention, work first and assets for the rest; the reverse is not available because holding assets does not change the support ratio. Counted this way the second dividend is smaller: 0.08 a year on average across the 165 economies for the window starting 2025 (Pre −0.02, Early 0.02, Late 0.09, Post 0.13; China 0.12, India 0.01, Japan 0.13), and it is negative for 38 economies, where longer working lives cover more than the whole rise in the pot.

Reading the time series

Each point is the window starting that year, so the value at 2025 describes 2025 to 2040 and the series ends at the window 2070 to 2085. All three dividends are driven by the age structure changing. Once a transition is complete and the age structure settles, anything that depends on it changing goes to zero: the second and third converge towards zero from above, the first from below, because the last phase of a transition is the old-age share settling. The late-century values rest on WPP's assumptions that fertility converges and mortality improvements slow, so they are the least certain.

For Asia-Pacific the picture is a handover rather than a disappearance. The first dividend closes this decade for South Asia (+0.28 for the window starting 2025, zero by 2035) and ASEAN (+0.01 to −0.18), while the second and third hold or grow until the 2050s; East Asia's first dividend is already deep in negative territory and bottoms at −0.97 for the window starting 2040. Youth bulges later in the century exist but not in Asia-Pacific: 44 economies still have a first dividend above +0.1 for the window starting 2050 and 24 for 2070, almost all in Sub-Saharan Africa (Chad, Central African Republic, DR Congo, Niger, Mali) plus Afghanistan; the Pacific Islands stay positive into the 2060s and Central Asia has a shallow second rise around 2040 to 2060. Kinks for start years around 2005 come from the fall in life expectancy in 2020 and 2021, which sits at the end of those windows.

Coverage and groupings

All three are potential contributions, not forecasts of realised growth. Coverage is the 165 economies with NTA profiles (95.6% of the world's population in 2025); the other 71 have no profile and are not shown, the largest being Tanzania, Myanmar, Sudan, Yemen, North Korea and Syria. Asia-Pacific subregions follow UN M49, with South Asia as the eight SAARC members and ASEAN as the eleven members including Timor-Leste (Myanmar has no profile). Other regions follow World Bank regions, with Afghanistan and Pakistan in South Asia and the Central Asian economies in Asia-Pacific. Income groups and stages are those of 2025. Sources: UN World Population Prospects 2024; UN Population Division NTA profiles; GMR typology (revised); Mason and Lee (2006), Mason, Lee, Abrigo and Lee (2017).

A measure no longer used: assets alone

Earlier versions of this page, and the 30 October brief on the Part 1 charts, carried the second dividend as the literature usually reports it: the same Mason-Lee pot counted on today's earning profile, with no allowance for older people working longer ("assets alone"). We have moved on from that measure because it overlaps the third dividend: the pot it counts includes the deficit years that the third dividend assumes are worked, so the two columns could not be read together, let alone added. On that measure the second dividend averaged 0.28 a year across the 165 economies for the window starting 2025 (Pre 0.11, Early 0.27, Late 0.33, Post 0.37; China 0.42, India 0.29, Japan 0.37), against 0.08 on the measure used here; the ranking of economies is similar (rank correlation 0.80). The series is still computed and kept in the data file (d2, beside d2_silver) for reference.
